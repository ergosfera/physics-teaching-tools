Signal Studio

About Signal Studio

Listen to music. See the signal. Understand the sound.

The story

Signal Studio began with a simple idea: recreate the pleasure of watching a realistic cassette turn while music plays. Inspired by the cassette animations in Casse-o-player and the appearance of vintage tapes, the project grew through a conversation about design, playback, and sound.

A deck became a player, then a small sound laboratory. Stereo meters, waveforms, a spectrum, and an equalizer brought the invisible signal into view. The central question became: what do these moving shapes and numbers tell us about what we hear?

The aim

This is a hands-on learning space for exploring amplitude, frequency, dynamics, decibels, and the relationship between digital audio and physical sound. Familiar music provides the material: change a control, listen, observe, and connect the result to an explanation.

What you can explore

  • Compare sustained energy with short peaks using the stereo meters.
  • Connect the waveform’s amplitude and timing with the spectrum’s frequency content.
  • Use the equalizer to hear and see how frequency balance changes.
  • Open each module’s information guide to understand its units, references, and limits.

The retro appearance makes the experience inviting, while the guides distinguish digital readings from calibrated acoustic measurements. Signal Studio is an educational player, not a calibrated sound-level meter.

SOUND LAB · MODULE GUIDE

Understanding sound

The needles show the sustained strength of the left and right signals. They help you see musical dynamics: how energy builds, falls, and differs between channels.

What does VU mean?

VU means volume unit. A VU scale compares a signal with a chosen operating level. Its zero means “at the reference level,” not silence and not the maximum possible level. Traditional VU meters respond relatively slowly—about 300 milliseconds for their specified response to a steady tone—so brief transients can be much higher than the needle suggests. Reference: ITU report on audio metering.

Why −20 through +3?

Those are the endpoints chosen for this dial, following a familiar VU-style scale. −20 VU is not the quietest audible sound. It represents one tenth of the reference amplitude. +3 VU is about 1.41 times that amplitude, or about twice its mean-square value. Anything below or above the dial’s range stays at the corresponding end of the needle travel. A positive VU value means “above nominal,” not automatically digital clipping.

The exact reference in this player

This is a VU-style RMS visualization, not a standards-compliant VU instrument. It computes the mean of the squared samples for each channel, smooths that value, takes its square root, and converts it to decibels. RMS means root mean square. Here the display is:

VU reading = 20 log₁₀(smoothed RMS / 1) + 11

The header’s “0 VU = −11 dBFS” uses this raw RMS convention. Other meters may calibrate RMS against a full-scale sine wave instead, giving a 3.01 dB difference. Always check the reference before comparing instruments.

Why do these needles have so much groove?

This version deliberately uses fast power-smoothing time constants: approximately 45 ms on rising levels and 180 ms on falling levels, on top of the sample-analysis window. These are exponential time constants, not times to reach the final value. The animation emphasizes musical rhythm; it does not implement traditional VU ballistics. The dial spacing is also nonlinear: a decibel step represents a ratio, not an equal addition of amplitude.

How to relate it to physical sound

RMS is useful because acoustic intensity is proportional to mean-square pressure under traveling-wave conditions. But these RMS samples are dimensionless. A needle at 0 VU does not specify watts, W/m², or dB SPL. The amplifier, speaker, distance, and room still determine the acoustic output. L and R identify channels; different readings indicate different channel energy, not the physical direction of an acoustic wave.

Try it

Play a track with isolated drum hits, then one with a sustained instrument. Compare the needles with the peak bars. A transient can raise a peak sharply without sustaining a high needle reading. Halving master volume should lower a steady signal by approximately 6.02 dB, until the dial reaches its lower stop.

The horizontal bars show sample peaks. Use them to see short bursts and how close the digital signal gets to full scale.

What is dBFS?

dBFS means decibels relative to full scale. In this meter, a sample magnitude of 1 is the reference. Positive and negative samples both count: a sample of −0.8 has the same peak magnitude as +0.8. The level calculation is:

Sample-peak level = 20 log₁₀(maximum |sample| / 1)

Limits, colors, and the little marker

The bar spans −60 to 0 dBFS. Below the display floor, its readout says “≤−60”; that does not prove silence. Green segments represent lower levels, amber begins near −12 dBFS, and red near −3 dBFS. These colors indicate digital headroom, not acoustic safety or hearing thresholds.

The pale marker holds the recent peak for about one second, then falls. The bar itself falls at approximately 32 dB per second so that a quick hit remains visible. The numeric readout follows that falling display value; it is not an instantaneous pressure measurement.

Zero, overload, and missed peaks

0 dBFS is the nominal sample ceiling for a full-scale PCM output. Floating-point processing can temporarily exceed magnitude 1 after EQ boosts. This bar stops visually at 0, although its numeric value can become positive. A later output stage may clip those overshoots, changing the waveform and adding distortion.

This implementation looks for peaks in the most recent 4,096-sample analysis buffer, refreshed about 30 times per second. It is an educational display, not a guaranteed continuous peak recorder: browser stalls or very high sample rates can cause events to be missed. It also does not reconstruct the signal between samples, so it is not a true-peak meter. Reconstructed peaks can be higher than the sampled peaks. Reference: ITU discussion of peak and true-peak meters.

Peak is different from average and loudness

A brief click and a sustained sound may share the same peak but have very different RMS levels and perceived loudness. The difference between peak and RMS levels is called the crest factor. For an ideal sine wave, RMS equals peak divided by √2, so the difference is approximately 3.01 dB. This explains why peak and VU-style readings should not be expected to match.

Try it

Watch a transient-rich track. Note the peak marker while the needles fall. Then lower master volume from 100% to 50%: an unchanged, steady signal falls by about 6.02 dB. That percentage controls linear signal amplitude, not a percentage of perceived loudness or a known number of acoustic watts.

A waveform shows the signal changing through time. Its height describes amplitude; the spacing of repeated patterns describes frequency.

Reading the two axes

Each channel spans 20 milliseconds (0.020 seconds) from left to right. Vertically, +1 and −1 are normalized digital sample amplitudes; 0 is the zero-signal line. Negative amplitude is the opposite polarity, not negative loudness or negative energy.

If a calibrated microphone had recorded the signal, polarity could be mapped to pressure fluctuations above and below the ambient pressure. In this player there is no pressure calibration, so the vertical axis is neither pascals nor W/m².

From cycles to hertz

Frequency f is the number of cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). The period T is the time required for one cycle:

f = 1 / T   ·   cycles in this window = f × 0.020

Real music contains many frequencies at once. Its waveform usually cannot be interpreted as a single repeating tone; the spectrum is better for separating those components. A louder version of the same signal grows vertically without necessarily changing the spacing of its cycles.

Amplitude, RMS, and energy

Simply averaging positive and negative samples can give zero even for a strong tone. RMS avoids cancellation by squaring every sample before averaging, then taking a square root. For a sine wave with peak amplitude A, RMS = A/√2. A sine with peaks of ±0.5 has RMS ≈0.354, a peak level of −6.02 dBFS, and a raw RMS level of −9.03 dB relative to amplitude 1.

What this drawing can and cannot show

The drawing searches for a rising zero crossing to stabilize each channel, then draws the minimum and maximum samples within each horizontal pixel. Dense high frequencies therefore look like a band, not individually resolved cycles. Values beyond ±1 are cropped by the drawing; a flat edge may indicate only the display boundary, so check the peak readout before concluding that the file is clipped.

The channels are aligned independently. Do not use their horizontal alignment to measure inter-channel phase or delay. The display is a short, repeatedly refreshed view, not a recording of the whole song.

Try it

Compare a sustained bass note with a bright, noisy passage. Follow the cycle spacing in the first and the dense texture in the second. Lower master volume: the waveform becomes shorter vertically while the pitch-related spacing remains essentially unchanged.

The spectrum shows how the signal is distributed across frequencies. It is a frequency view of the same digital audio shown by the waveform.

Frequency, pitch, and the audible range

The horizontal axis runs from 20 Hz to 20 kHz; 1 kHz means 1,000 cycles per second. It uses logarithmic spacing: equal distances represent equal frequency ratios. Doubling frequency is one octave, so 100–200 Hz and 1,000–2,000 Hz span the same distance.

About 20 Hz–20 kHz is a conventional range for young human hearing, not a guarantee for every listener. Sensitivity depends on frequency and the person, and the upper limit commonly decreases with age. Frequencies below and above that range are called infrasonic and ultrasonic. A plotted component is not proof that you can hear it. Reference: National Academies, hearing basics.

What the bars actually measure

This player computes a 4,096-sample FFT (fast Fourier transform) for each channel and groups its results into 32 logarithmically spaced bands. For each band it averages the squared FFT magnitudes over its included bins and over L and R, then expresses that average logarithmically. It does not add L and R waveforms together, so opposite channel polarities do not cancel in this display.

The vertical scale is −84 to −12 dB. These are display limits, not the limits of hearing or the clipping ceiling. Tall bars stop at the upper boundary even if their selected numeric value is higher. The pale caps are recent peak markers. The highlighted line identifies the selected band; it is a cursor, not another signal. Hover, tap, or use the slider’s arrow keys to inspect it; Escape clears the selection.

Why this does not equal the peak meter

These values are relative FFT levels, not a calibrated tone-amplitude dBFS measurement, SPL, or spectral density in W/m²/Hz. The browser applies a Blackman window and frequency smoothing. Windowing changes the amplitude and spread of a tone; this app does not compensate for that. Reference: W3C, FFT windowing and smoothing.

Additionally, this app averages bins instead of summing total band energy. Wide high-frequency bands contain more bins, so an isolated tone can look lower when averaged across a wider band. Comparing bar heights is useful qualitatively, but adding their dB values cannot produce total intensity. The frequency in the selection readout is the band’s geometric center, not an exact detected musical note.

Resolution and sampling limits

FFT bin spacing is sample rate ÷ 4,096: about 11.72 Hz at 48 kHz, or 10.77 Hz at 44.1 kHz. At 48 kHz the analysis window lasts about 85.3 ms. Closely spaced low-frequency bands can share bins; they are not independent fine measurements. Windowing also limits how well nearby tones can be distinguished.

The Nyquist frequency is half the sample rate. At 48 kHz it is 24 kHz, though this plot ends at 20 kHz. If the active audio context uses a lower sample rate, frequencies above its Nyquist limit cannot be represented. The context may resample the media, so its analysis rate need not equal the original file’s rate.

Try it

Play a sustained note and look for a fundamental plus harmonics near whole-number multiples of it. Raise the 1 kHz EQ fader slightly: nearby spectrum bands should rise when that region is present in the music. Lower master volume and the whole spectrum falls without shifting its frequencies.

The equalizer changes the balance of frequencies. Its faders control gain, while the spectrum and meters show the resulting signal.

Ten center frequencies

The bands are centered at 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500 Hz, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 kHz. Short labels round the first two values. Each center is twice the previous one—one octave apart. Low bands affect bass, middle bands much of the body of voices and instruments, and high bands brightness and fine detail. These descriptions overlap; instruments contain many harmonics.

Gain in dB, not dBFS

A fader describes a change relative to its input, not an absolute output level. Its range is −12 to +12 dB in 0.5 dB steps. At 0 dB that filter applies no boost or cut. At its center frequency, the amplitude multiplier for a gain G is:

Amplitude multiplier = 10^(G / 20)

Why neighboring frequencies move too

These are ten cascading peaking filters, with Q set to 1.4. Each affects a region around its center, not a single isolated frequency. Their responses overlap; the combined response depends on all ten settings, and its peak can exceed the boost of one fader. Near the Nyquist limit, the upper bands are constrained by the sample rate.

A +6 dB boost at 1 kHz does not guarantee that the whole song rises by 6 dB. It depends on how much of the song occupies the affected region. The total waveform, RMS, peaks, and perceived timbre can all change. Filters can also change phase and transient shape; they do not simply redraw spectrum bars.

Equalizer, master volume, and bypass

The current path is media → master volume → ten EQ filters → stereo meters and output. All monitoring displays therefore reflect the processed signal. The operating-system or hardware volume is later in the chain and is not measured here.

Moving an EQ fader no longer triggers automatic overall attenuation. Master volume is independent: 50% multiplies samples by 0.5, about −6.02 dB; 0% mutes. Reducing a band can still lower the overall level naturally because energy has been removed. Boosts can push peaks beyond full scale; use the peak meter to observe this and reduce master volume if necessary.

BYPASS temporarily sets all filter gains to zero while preserving your chosen settings. FLAT resets those settings to zero. Neither changes master volume.

Relation to acoustic intensity

For a single affected tone and an unchanged, linear playback system, +6 dB gain gives about twice the acoustic pressure and four times the intensity at the same location. This is a relative prediction, not a measurement of actual W/m². Speaker response, room acoustics, compression, and distortion can alter the result.

Try it

Start FLAT. Raise only the 250 Hz fader by 3 dB and watch the spectrum while listening to the tonal change. Toggle BYPASS to compare the same passage, then restore the EQ. A louder result is not automatically a clearer one; compare at similar overall loudness when judging tone.

Connecting the display to physical sound

Decibels: a ratio needs a reference

A decibel (dB) expresses a ratio on a logarithmic scale. For power or intensity use 10 log₁₀(value/reference). For pressure or signal amplitude use 20 log₁₀(value/reference), when comparing quantities whose powers are proportional to their squares under the same conditions.

Thus +3.01 dB means twice the intensity but about 1.414 times the pressure; +6.02 dB means four times the intensity and twice the pressure. A negative value means below the reference, not negative physical energy. Zero means equal to the reference. Reference: National Physical Laboratory, decibel scale.

“dB” alone is incomplete for an absolute level: EQ dB is a gain ratio; dBFS refers to digital full scale; dB SPL refers to acoustic pressure. The same number in these different systems does not describe the same thing.

Watts, W/m², pascals, and dB SPL

Acoustic power is energy transferred per second, in watts (W). Intensity is sound energy flow per unit area, in watts per square metre (W/m²). Sound pressure is a fluctuation in pressure, measured in pascals (Pa). An amplifier’s electrical wattage is not the same as radiated acoustic power.

Intensity level Lᵢ = 10 log₁₀(I / 10⁻¹² W/m²)
Pressure level Lₚ = 20 log₁₀(pᵣₘₛ / 20 µPa)

The pressure reference is for sound in air. Under progressive plane-wave conditions, I = pᵣₘₛ²/(ρc), where ρ is air density and c is sound speed. Pressure and intensity levels are then approximately equal with these references; this relation cannot be assumed in every room or near a source. Reference: NPL, pressure and intensity levels.

Why this player cannot directly display acoustic watts

A digital sample has no built-in conversion to pascals. The same −20 dBFS file can produce very different acoustic levels through different headphones, amplifier settings, speakers, distances, or rooms. These displays analyze the file after this player’s volume and EQ, before your hardware turns it into sound.

To connect digital RMS with dB SPL, measure the actual acoustic output using a calibrated measurement chain at a specified position, with playback settings fixed. A calibration for one tone and one setup does not establish the frequency response for all sounds. Converting pressure to intensity additionally requires appropriate sound-field assumptions or an intensity measurement system.

As a conditional example, if a steady tone has been measured at 70 dB SPL, halving its digital amplitude predicts roughly 64 dB SPL at the same position in a linear, unchanged system. No absolute SPL value can be inferred before that calibration.

Frequency, wavelength, and perceived loudness

Frequency counts cycles per second. Wavelength is the distance traveled in one cycle: λ = c/f. Using c ≈343 m/s in air near 20°C, a 100 Hz tone has a wavelength of about 3.43 m; a 1 kHz tone about 0.343 m. These are physical distances, not the horizontal width of the on-screen waveform.

Perceived loudness also depends on frequency, duration, spectrum, and the listener. Equal physical intensity at two frequencies need not sound equally loud. None of these displays implements A-weighted sound pressure, standardized integrated loudness in LUFS, or a calibrated hearing test. Further reading: Macquarie University, basic acoustics.
