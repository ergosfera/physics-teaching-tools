What to notice

Most sounds are not a single frequency. They are a mixture of several frequencies, each with its own strength. The spectrum shows that mixture: each peak is one frequency in the sound.

The strongest components decide what you hear. Much weaker ones are still there, but they are barely perceivable. Try the Loud + faint preset: the faint frequency is easy to see in decibels, almost invisible on the linear axis, and hard to hear.

The lowest strong peak of a musical sound is its fundamental and sets the pitch. The others (harmonics) set its timbre: why a clarinet and a flute playing the same note sound different.

About decibels

Each 20 dB down means 10 times less amplitude: −20 dB is 1/10, −40 dB is 1/100, −60 dB is 1/1000. The ear works roughly this way too, which is why the decibel axis looks closer to what you hear than the linear one.

What to notice

Humans hear from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Below 20 Hz is infrasound (elephants, earthquakes); above 20 kHz is ultrasound (bats, dog whistles, medical imaging).

The upper limit drops with age. Many teenagers still hear 17 kHz; many adults stop around 12–15 kHz. Compare your results with your classmates'.

Your speakers have limits too: small laptop speakers often can't produce anything below about 150 Hz. If the low tones vanish early, it may be the speaker, not your ear.

Two tones can carry the same energy and still not sound equally loud: the ear is most sensitive around 2–5 kHz and much less sensitive to low and very high frequencies.

How to read the graph

Each curve joins sounds that are perceived as equally loud. Its loudness in phon equals its level in decibels at 1 kHz.

Follow the 40-phon curve: at 1 kHz it needs 40 dB, but at 100 Hz it needs about 62 dB to sound just as loud. The dip near 3–4 kHz is where your ear is most sensitive: the ear canal resonates there.

The lowest curve is the threshold of hearing: anything below it is too faint to hear at all. Hover over the graph to read the loudness of any frequency and level.

What to notice

Sound is a longitudinal wave: the air particles move back and forth along the direction the wave travels. Watch the red particle: it never travels down the tube. What travels is the pattern of compressions and rarefactions.

The wavelength λ is the distance between two compressions, read on the snapshot. The period T is the time between two compressions arriving at the microphone. In one period the wave advances one wavelength, so v = λ / T = f · λ.

Change the frequency: λ changes, but v doesn't. The speed depends on the medium and its temperature, not on the frequency.

About the simulation

The animation runs in slow motion: real sound waves vibrate hundreds of times per second. The particle motion is greatly exaggerated; in real sound it is far smaller than a millimeter.

Speeds used: air 331.3 m/s·√(1 + T/273), helium 972 m/s and carbon dioxide 259 m/s at 0 °C with the same temperature factor, and water from an empirical formula (about 1480 m/s at 20 °C).
