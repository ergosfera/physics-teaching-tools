What to notice

A hot object (a bulb filament, the Sun, embers) gives a continuous spectrum: every wavelength, with a peak that moves to shorter wavelengths as it gets hotter (Wien's law, λpeak = 2.898 mm·K / T). Look at the bulb with UV and infrared shown: most of its power is invisible heat.

A hot gas (hydrogen, neon, sodium) gives only a few bright lines. Each element has its own set of lines, like a fingerprint. In sunlight the same lines appear dark: cooler gas in the Sun's atmosphere absorbs them.

Very different spectra can look the same color. Compare Sodium with the Red + green mix, or the white LED with sunlight: the eye only has three kinds of color sensors, so it can't tell them apart.

How to make a spectroscope

Simplest: hold a piece of a CD or DVD (or a diffraction-grating film) right in front of the phone or laptop camera, tilted, and aim it at a lamp. Move it until a rainbow appears on the screen.

Better: a small box or tube, black inside, with a narrow slit (two pieces of aluminium foil or tape almost touching) at one end and the grating at the other. Narrow slits give sharp lines.

Calibrate with a fluorescent lamp or a CFL: its brightest lines are blue 435.8 nm and green 546.1 nm (mercury) and red 611.6 nm (europium). After that, look at a white LED, a bulb, a phone screen, a sodium street lamp or sunlight on a white sheet (never at the Sun directly).

Phone cameras adjust exposure and colors by themselves, so heights are only approximate. If a peak looks flat on top, the camera is saturated: dim the light or move away.

About wavelength, frequency and energy

Light is an electromagnetic wave. Its speed in vacuum is c = 299 792 458 m/s, so f = c / λ: visible light vibrates about 500 trillion times per second. Each photon carries an energy E = h · f; violet photons carry about twice the energy of red ones.

Compare with sound. We hear from 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz: the highest frequency is 1000 times the lowest (about 10 octaves). We see from about 750 nm to 380 nm: the highest frequency is only about twice the lowest. All the colors fit in less than one "octave" of light.
Try it: see the invisible. Point a TV remote at your phone's camera and press a button. The camera shows a flashing light that your eyes can't see: the remote uses infrared at about 940 nm. (Some phones block infrared on the main camera; try the selfie camera.)
What to notice

Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all the same kind of wave, traveling at the same speed c. Only the wavelength (and so the frequency and photon energy) changes.

The visible range is tiny. Our eyes are tuned to it because it is where the Sun gives the most light and where water (in the air and in our eyes) is transparent.

Photons with more energy than a few electronvolts (ultraviolet and beyond) can break molecules apart: that is why UV causes sunburn and X-rays need protection, while radio waves don't, however strong.

Why can't the screen show this wavelength?

A speaker can play any frequency, but a screen can't emit any wavelength: each pixel only has a red, a green and a blue light. The colors shown here are the closest mixtures the screen can make. Pure spectral colors (a laser, a sodium lamp) look more vivid in real life than on any screen.

Two lights can carry the same power and still not look equally bright: in daylight the eye is most sensitive to yellow-green light near 555 nm and much less to deep red or violet.

What to notice

The daylight curve peaks at 555 nm. A green laser looks far brighter than a red or blue laser of the same power, which is why green laser pointers are the most visible.

In the dark, the eye switches to the rods: the curve moves to 507 nm and colors disappear (rods don't see color). Red flowers look almost black at dusk while leaves still look light. This is the Purkinje effect.

The three cone curves overlap a lot. Every color you see is the brain comparing how strongly these three are excited.

About the experiment

Your screen's green light contributes the most to brightness and its blue light the least. The standard for screens says white = 21% red + 72% green + 7% blue in brightness. These numbers come straight from the eye's sensitivity curve.

Slow motion: the animation is slowed down about 1015 times. Real light vibrates about 500 trillion times per second.

What to notice

Light is a transverse wave: the electric field points across the direction of travel. Nothing material moves along; the field pattern travels.

When light enters the material it slows down. The frequency stays the same (the detector sees the same period everywhere), so the wavelength gets shorter: v = f · λ. The color is set by the frequency, so the light keeps its color.

The refractive index n = c / v says how many times slower light travels. Because light slows down, it bends when it enters at an angle (refraction). Change the color: in glass, violet light is a little slower than red light, which is why a prism splits white light into a rainbow (dispersion).

About the simulation

Real light also partly reflects at the surface and its amplitude changes a little; that is left out here. The refractive indices depend on the wavelength following Cauchy's formula, with values close to real ones: water 1.333, crown glass 1.517, diamond 2.418 for yellow light (589 nm). Air (1.0003) is so close to vacuum that the difference can't be measured on the graphs.
