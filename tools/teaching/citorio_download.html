💬 Citorio

Every so often a small window pops up over whatever you are doing with a quote, a saying or a joke, then hides again. It comes with 1,315 quotes in Spanish, and you can add your own categories and lists.

The quote window on a Mac. It floats over other windows without taking the keyboard, and goes away by itself.

1About

What it does

  • Lives by the clock: in the notification area on Windows and in the menu bar on a Mac. Every minute (you choose how often) it shows a quote in a small window that stays on top without taking the keyboard, so you can keep typing.
  • Longer quotes stay up longer (10 to 40 seconds, adjustable). Moving the mouse over the quote holds it. Closing the window only hides it until the next quote.
  • Buttons: Next, Later (hide now and show the same quote next time), Pause, Copy and Settings. Opening Citorio again while it runs shows a quote right away.
  • Quotes are set in a serif font (Georgia unless you pick another), with an icon for each category.
  • It doesn't repeat itself: a quote doesn't come back until most of the others have been shown.
  • Never hide mode goes straight from one quote to the next, for a projector or a second screen.

Your own categories and quotes

  • All the quotes are plain-text files in Documents/Citorio: one file per category. Add a file to add a category; edit a file in Notepad or TextEdit to add quotes. Citorio picks up changes by itself.
  • In a file, quotes are separated by a line with only %, and a last line starting with — (or --) is the author. A simple list with one quote per line also works.
  • # name: and # icon: at the top of a file name the category and choose its icon, for example # icon: 🔭.
  • Settings turns categories on and off and has buttons for New category, Import list (any .txt file becomes a category), Edit, Open folder and Restore built-in lists.

The quotes it comes with

A tribute to Citorio 1.1 (1995–96), the freeware quote program for Windows 3.1 by Javier Cámara Melgosa, itself based on Pundit (1990) by Jeff Bienstadt. Its 1,315 quotes in Spanish (Bierce's Devil's Dictionary, Wilde, Twain, Groucho Marx, Murphy's laws, exam answers and many more) are the ones he collected and translated, in nine categories: Generales, Divertidas, Maliciosas, Deprimentes, Ley de Murphy, Románticas, Dichas por el ordenador, Animosas and De vagos. For use at school, the original's category of sex jokes is left out, along with other quotes with sexual content, vulgar language, or that make fun of a religion, a people or women. Many of the rest are from the 1990s and earlier; turn off any category, or edit its file. This program is new: the code and icons are not from the original.

Privacy

Citorio collects no personal data and never connects to the internet. It only reads the quote files in your Documents folder. Its options are kept in your user settings on your computer.

Installing

  • Windows: double-click Citorio.exe. No installation is needed. The app isn't signed with a paid certificate, so the first time Windows may show “Windows protected your PC”. Click More info, then Run anyway. Its icon appears by the clock (it may be under the ˄ arrow); right-click it for Settings and Quit. Start with Windows is in the same menu.
  • macOS: open Citorio.dmg and drag Citorio into Applications. The app isn't signed with a paid Apple Developer ID, so the first time macOS may block it. Open System Settings → Privacy & Security and click Open Anyway. macOS asks once to let Citorio use your Documents folder, where it keeps the quotes. It has no Dock icon: its menu is the speech bubble in the menu bar, with Settings, Open at Login and Quit.
