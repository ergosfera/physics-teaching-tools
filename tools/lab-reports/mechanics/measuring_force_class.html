Station 2 ≈ 12 minWhat is a force?

A force is always one object acting on another. Watch four interactions. The red arrow is the force on one object, the purple one the force back on the other. Then answer one question for each scene.

AnimationFour interactions

So, a force is… an interaction between two objects: a push or a pull. It can change the motion of an object (speed it up, slow it down, turn it) or change its shape. Forces always come in pairs, the same size in opposite directions, one on each object. Some need contact, others (magnets, gravity) act at a distance.

Station 3 ≈ 12 minThe newton: how big is 1 N?

A fan cart sits on an air track with no friction. The fan pushes it with a constant force F for 2 seconds, and the graph shows its speed. Mission: make the 1 kg cart speed up by exactly 1 m/s every second.

AnimationFan cart on an air track

F (N)m (kg)speed after 2 sspeed gained each second

DefinitionThe newton

1 N = 1 kg · 1 ms²

One newton is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²: its speed grows by 1 m/s every second. It is named after Isaac Newton, and it is the SI unit of force.

Feel itWhat do things weigh in newtons?

On Earth, every kilogram is pulled down with about 9.8 N. A small apple (about 102 g) weighs about 1 N: hold an apple and you feel one newton.

DefinitionThe kilogram-force

1 kgf = weight of 1 kg where g = 9.80665 ms² ≈ 9.8 N

It is an older unit, still common on gym machines, tyre gauges and hand dynamometers. It is not SI: in physics we use newtons.

Now: what does a bathroom scale really measure, and why does it show “kg”? Would it be right on the Moon? Correct yourselves if you need to.

A bathroom scale measures a force: how hard you push down on it. Inside, a spring (or a strain sensor) is squeezed. The maker divides that force by 9.8 N/kg so the screen shows “kg”. That is fine on Earth, where your weight in kgf has the same number as your mass in kg. On the Moon, a 60 kg student pushes with only about 10 kgf, so the scale shows “10 kg”. The force it reads is right; the mass it claims is not. A pan balance compares masses, so it works on any world.

🔎 How does the spring know the force?

The stretch of a good spring is proportional to the force: twice the force, twice the stretch (Hooke's law, F = k·x). So equal steps of force are equal steps on the scale, and the maker can print the marks evenly.

This one stretches 1 cm for each newton, so k = 100 N/m. Past its range the spring is damaged: it stays longer and stops being proportional.

Station 6 ≈ 14 minReading it right: precision and error

No measurement is perfect. Here you learn to write a reading with its uncertainty, avoid parallax, choose the right dynamometer, and tell precision from accuracy. Do the four parts in order.

Magnified scaleRead where the red pointer is

Reading: ± N

How toWrite a reading from an analog scale

  1. Find the smallest division: the value between two neighbouring marks.
  2. Read the mark nearest the pointer. If it is right in the middle of two marks, you may give the half.
  3. The uncertainty is half of the smallest division.
  4. Write (value ± uncertainty) unit, with the value rounded to the same decimal place as the uncertainty.
e.g. divisions of 0.1 N → (2.35 ± 0.05) N

Read three different scales right to finish this part.

AnimationWhere is your eye?

The pointer is a little in front of the scale. Move your eye up and down: the pointer seems to sit on a different mark. This is called parallax. Some dynamometers have a mirror strip behind the pointer: when the pointer hides its own reflection, your eye is level.

AnimationThe same 0.80 N on two dynamometers

Relative errorHow big is the uncertainty compared with the value?

relative error = uncertaintyvalue × 100 %
0 – 1 N dynamometer: (0.80 ± 0.01) N%
0 – 10 N dynamometer: (0.8 ± 0.1) N%
Same object, but the uncertainty is 10 times smaller on the 0 – 1 N dynamometer. Use the smallest range that still holds the load: its divisions are finer. Never go over the range.

AnimationFour dynamometers weigh the same 2.00 N stone, ten times each

The dashed line is the true value, 2.00 N.

Two different ideasPrecision and accuracy

Precise: the readings are close to each other (little scatter).
Accurate: the readings are close to the true value (on average).

Random errors scatter the readings both ways: shaking hands, a pointer that won't settle. Systematic errors push every reading the same way: a dynamometer that isn't zeroed, a damaged spring, always reading from above.
