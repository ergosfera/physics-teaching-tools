💬 Wait a moment…

On the merry-go-round, the bigger radius needed more force. In the car, the smaller radius needed more force. Can both be right?

Hint: what stays the same in each case? Try it: in the simulator switch between “keep the same period” and “keep the same speed”, and move the radius.

Both are right. Same period (merry-go-round, everybody turns together): a bigger circle means a faster ball, and the force grows with r. Same speed (car at 40 km/h): a bigger circle bends the path more gently, and the force drops with r. “Bigger r → bigger force?” has no answer until you say what you keep fixed.

Light mass m = 30 g goes around. Heavy mass M = 150 g hangs below, so its weight is Mg = 1.47 N. String from the top of the tube to the heavy mass: 1.00 m. Keep the heavy mass at the mark: the timer counts 10 laps by itself.

Think firstWho pulls the light mass toward the center?

The only thing touching it that can pull it toward the center is the string. The tension is the centripetal force: “centripetal” is not a new kind of force, it is the job the tension is doing.
While the heavy mass hangs still, how big is the tension?
The heavy mass is at rest: string up, weight down, so they must be equal. T = Mg. If it moves up or down, they are not equal any more: that's why it has to stay still.

Your labWhat probably went wrong in YOUR group's lab?

🔧 Possible reasons our real lab went wrong
  • The heavy mass wasn't still. If it moves up or down, the tension isn't Mg any more.
  • Friction in the tube. The string rubs on the edge, so the pull at the top isn't exactly the weight below.
  • Timing. F depends on 1/T²: a 3 % error in T becomes a 6 % error in F. Being 0.3 s off over 10 short laps is easy.
  • The radius. Pinching the string late, or measuring to the edge instead of the center of the mass, changes r.
  • Units. Grams instead of kilograms gives forces 1000 times too big.

Nice fact: the string never is exactly horizontal (the light mass has weight too). But if you use the string length as r, the tension still comes out right.

Station 3Up in the sky: the same rule, bigger strings

The Moon goes around the Earth in a circle (almost). Something must be pulling it toward the center, like the string. We can measure how hard, with nothing more than r, T and our formula. No new law needed.

v = 2πr/T and a = v²/r. Work them out in your notebook, then tap show.

Why does your own row break the pattern in the last column? You aren't in orbit: the ground holds you up too. You only need 0.034 m/s² toward the center, so at the equator a scale reads you about 0.3 % lighter.

🌕 Tie the Moon to the EarthHow strong would the rope be?

The Moon's mass is 7.35 × 1022 kg. If gravity were a rope, its tension would be the centripetal force F = m·a.

F = 7.35 × 1022 kg × 0.00272 m/s² ≈ 2.0 × 1020 N. A steel cable that strong would be about 700 km thick. Gravity does it with no cable at all.
Now imagine a Moon twice as heavy, same distance, same 27.3 days. What changes?
a = 4π²r/T² has no mass in it. A heavier Moon needs a stronger pull, but goes around in exactly the same way. A pebble at the Moon's distance would also take 27.3 days. So the orbit can't tell us the Moon's mass, only how hard the Earth pulls on each kilogram there.
🍎 A clue Newton found

The apple falls with 9.8 m/s². The Moon “falls” toward the Earth with 0.00272 m/s². Divide: 9.8 / 0.00272 ≈ 3600 = 60 × 60.

And the Moon is about 60 Earth radii away. Sixty times farther, 60 × 60 times weaker. Look at the last column of the table: a·r² is the same for every satellite. That is a law hiding in the data: a story for next year.

🚀 AstronautsThe ISS accelerates at 8.7 m/s², almost g. So why do astronauts float?

Gravity is still there (almost 90 % of what you feel). The station and the astronauts fall together with the same acceleration around the Earth, so nothing pushes them against the floor. They are always falling, and always missing the Earth.

Station 4Who's watching? Centripetal is real, centrifugal depends on you

A puck is tied to the center of a spinning turntable. We watch it twice: from a camera on the ceiling, and from a camera riding on the turntable. Same puck, same moment. Two very different stories.

Before you cutIs the puck moving?

Ceiling camera: yes, in a circle. Its velocity keeps changing direction, so something must pull it in: the tension. One force, pointing to the center. Everything adds up.

Rider: no! The puck sits still next to them. But the string is clearly pulling it inward… To explain why it doesn't move, the rider invents a force pushing outward: centrifugal force.

After you cutWhich way does it go?

From the ceiling: a straight line, tangent to the circle. No force, so no change of velocity. The rider sees it curve away and says “centrifugal force threw it out”. Both describe the same puck. Only the ceiling camera needs no invented forces.

🚗 In the carThe car turns left and you slide to the right, against the door.

Nothing pushes you to the right. Your body just keeps going straight, while the car turns left under you. The door catches you and pushes you left, toward the center: that push is your centripetal force. “Being thrown outward” is the story told from inside the car, the rider's camera.

✍️ Your answerA friend says: “Centrifugal force threw the puck out when the string broke.” What do you answer?

💬 The big idea

Centripetal force is real: a string, a door, gravity, friction. Everybody agrees it is there. Centrifugal force only appears for someone turning with the object: it depends on the observer, and nothing in the world is doing the pushing.
