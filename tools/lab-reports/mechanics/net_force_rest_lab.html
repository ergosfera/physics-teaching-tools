Predict before you measure

A ring is held by two spring scales, one horizontal and one inclined, and a mass hangs from it. The ring stays at rest. Before touching anything, discuss with your partner:

The net force on the ring (all the forces added together) is…
Which spring scale will show the larger reading?
Why do you think so? Write the reason you both agree on.
3

Set up and read the forces

a · Set up (see the picture in the 📋 Lab guide)

  1. Place the two universal supports on the table, about 60 to 80 cm apart.
  2. Zero both spring scales: hold each one in the position it will have (one horizontal, one inclined) and adjust it until it reads 0 N with nothing hanging from it.
  3. Attach scale 1 to the left support and hook it to the ring. Scale 1 must be completely horizontal: move its clamp up or down until it is level.
  4. Attach scale 2 higher on the right support and hook it to the ring, so it is inclined.
  5. Hang the mass from the ring with a short piece of thread.
  6. Let everything settle. The ring must be at rest and touch only the two scales and the thread. The scales must not touch the supports or the table. Check again that scale 1 is still horizontal.

b · Read the forces

  1. Write the mass in grams: it is printed on the mass, or measure it with the balance.
  2. Read both scales with your eyes at the level of the pointer, without touching anything.
Don't take anything apart yet. You'll photograph this setup in the next step.
4

Photograph the setup and measure the angles

a · Take the photo

  1. Place the camera about 1 m in front of the setup, at the height of the ring, facing it straight on. The ring, the thread and both scales must be in the picture.
  2. Don't touch the setup. Take two or three photos and keep the sharpest one.
  3. Copy the photo to this computer.

b · Measure the angles in the Image Measurement Tool

📐 Open the Image Measurement ToolIt opens in a new tab, so this report stays open here.
The thread with the mass hangs exactly vertical: gravity pulls it straight down. That's why you measure every angle from the thread. It works even if the camera is a little tilted. You don't need to calibrate: angles don't need a scale.
  1. Click 📂 Import image and choose your photo. Use Zoom + or ⊞ Fit so the ring looks big.
  2. Click ∠ Angle, then click 3 points: a point on the thread below the ring, the center of the ring (the vertex), and a point on the line of scale 1. This is β₁.
  3. Click ∠ Angle again: thread → center of the ring → a point on the line of scale 2. This is β₂.
  4. Click ∠ Angle once more: scale 1 → center of the ring → scale 2. This is β₁₂.
  5. The angles appear in the Measurements list on the right. Click a name to rename it (β1, β2, β12).

5

The three forces on the ring

Three things pull on the ring: scale 1, scale 2, and the thread, which pulls down with the weight of the mass. To add the forces, you need each one's size and direction.

a · The weight of the mass

Use W = m · g with the mass in kilograms and g = 9.8 m/s².

b · The angle of each scale from the horizontal

The thread is vertical, so a horizontal line makes 90° with it. The angle of each scale above the horizontal is θ = β − 90°.

θ₁ should be close to 0° because scale 1 is horizontal. It comes out negative if scale 1 points a little below the horizontal: keep the minus sign.

c · Your free-body diagram

The diagram appears when you have the forces, the weight and both angles.

Look at the diagram. Describe each force on the ring: who pulls, in which direction, and how strongly.
6

Decompose each force into x and y

An inclined force has a horizontal part (x) and a vertical part (y). With the angle θ measured from the horizontal:

Fx = F · cos θ    Fy = F · sin θ

  • Choose the axes: x positive to the right, y positive upward.
  • A force that points left has a negative x part. A force that points down has a negative y part.
  • Set your calculator to degrees (DEG), not radians.
a · Scale 1: pulls to the left
b · Scale 2: pulls to the right and up
c · The weight: pulls straight down

The weight has no horizontal part, and its vertical part points down.

7

Find the net force

a · Add the parts on each axis

Forces on the same axis just add, with their signs.

b · The size of the net force

ΣFx and ΣFy are perpendicular, so use Pythagoras: |F net| = √(ΣFx² + ΣFy²).

c · The forces tip to tail

Each arrow starts where the previous one ends. If the net force is zero, the last arrow ends exactly where the first one started.

📋 Lab guide: Net Force at Rest

Read this together before you start. It tells you what you'll do in this lab; the detailed instructions are inside each step of the report. You can open it again at any moment with the 📋 Lab guide button at the top.

Goal

Hold a ring at rest with two spring scales and a hanging mass, measure the three forces on it and their angles, decompose each force into horizontal and vertical parts, and find the net force on the ring.

What you'll do

  1. Predict (step 2): decide what the net force on a ring at rest will be.
  2. Set up and read (step 3): build the setup in the picture and read both spring scales.
  3. Photograph and measure (step 4): take a photo, measure the angles in the Image Measurement Tool, and bring the angles and a screenshot to this report.
  4. Find the forces and their parts (steps 5 and 6): the weight, the angles from the horizontal, and the x and y parts of each force.
  5. Add them up (step 7): find the net force and discuss whether it is zero.
  6. Conclude and send (steps 8 and 9).

Materials

Each group
  • Two spring scales (newton meters)
  • A mass, for example 100 g or 200 g
  • Two universal supports with clamps
  • A small metal ring and a short piece of thread
  • A camera or phone, and a computer with this page and the Image Measurement Tool
Tips
  • A plain wall or a sheet of paper behind the ring makes the thread and the scales easy to see in the photo.
  • Take the photo straight in front of the ring, not from the side.
  • Read each scale with your eyes at the level of its pointer.
Take care: the supports can tip over when the scales pull on them. Keep their bases flat on the table, and don't stretch a scale past its maximum reading.
