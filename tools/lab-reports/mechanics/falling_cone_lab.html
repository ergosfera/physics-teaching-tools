Record and prepare the video

a · Set up

  1. Make the cone: cut a paper circle, cut a slit from the edge to the center, overlap the edges and tape them. It must be narrower than the tube so it falls without touching the walls.
  2. Fix the tube vertically to the support.
  3. Put two tape marks on the tube, one near the top and one near the bottom, and measure the distance between them with the meter stick (for example 90.0 cm). You'll use it to calibrate the video.
  4. Tape a white sheet behind the tube.
  5. Place the camera on a stable support 1.5 to 2 m away, at the height of the middle of the tube, facing it straight on (see the picture in the 📋 Lab guide). The whole tube and both marks must fit in the picture. Don't zoom or move the camera while recording.
  6. If your camera lets you choose, record at 60 frames per second. Write down the frame rate below.

b · Record

  1. Start recording, then hold the cone point down just above the top mark and let it go, without pushing it.
  2. Record three drops. Stop recording after each landing, or keep recording all three in one video.
  3. Watch the video: the cone must be visible all the way down and must not touch the walls. Choose the best drop.
  4. Copy the video to this computer (MP4 works best).

c · Load and trim in the Video Tracker

🎬 Open the Video TrackerIt opens in a new tab, so this report stays open here.
  1. 1 Load Video: type the frame rate of your video in FPS first, then click 📂 Load and choose your video.
  2. 2 Trim: use ◀ Frame / Frame ▶ to find the frame where the cone is released and click ▶In. Find the frame where it reaches the bottom mark and click Out◀. If your video has several drops, trim around the best one.
  3. Write both frame numbers below, then click Done ✓ in the Video Tracker.

Method 2: average velocity v = Δy/Δt

a · The whole fall

Open the Data Table in the Video Tracker and read the first and the last tracked points. Calculate v = (y₂ − y₁) / (t₂ − t₁) yourself.

b · Does v change during the fall?

Your data is split into four parts of the fall. Calculate the average velocity Δy/Δt in each part.

💬 Stop and discuss
Compare your four velocities. Does v change during the fall? How big is the change Δv compared with v itself? Is that change bigger than what your clicking and measuring errors could produce?

📋 Lab guide: Falling Cone

Read this together before you start. It tells you what you'll do in this lab; the detailed instructions are inside each step of the report. You can open it again at any moment with the 📋 Lab guide button at the top.

Goal

Measure the velocity of a paper cone falling inside a vertical tube with two methods (the slope of the position graph, and the definition v = Δy/Δt), and decide whether the cone falls with uniform velocity.

What you'll do

  1. Predict (step 2): decide how the cone's speed will change while it falls.
  2. Set up and record (step 3): build the cone, film it falling through the tube, and trim the video in the Video Tracker.
  3. Track (step 4): calibrate the video, click the cone in every frame, and bring the data and a screenshot to this report.
  4. Find the velocity with two methods (steps 5 and 6): the slope of the position graph, and v = Δy/Δt for the whole fall and for four parts of it. You'll stop to discuss how v changes.
  5. Compare, decide if the motion is uniform, conclude and send (steps 7 to 9).

Materials

Each pair
  • A paper cone (or paper, scissors and tape to make one)
  • A clear plastic tube, 1 meter long, held vertical
  • Tape for marks, and a meter stick
  • A camera that records video, on a stable support
  • A computer with this page and the Video Tracker
Tips
  • A white sheet of paper behind the tube makes the cone easy to see.
  • Good light keeps the cone sharp in every frame.
  • The cone must fall freely, without touching the walls of the tube.
Take care: release the cone gently and keep the tube steady. Don't climb on chairs or tables to reach the top of the tube: lower the support instead.
