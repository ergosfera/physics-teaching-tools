3

Find the points with 1 V, 2 V, 3 V …

a · Connect (student 1)

  1. Choose the arrangement you'll measure first (the tabs on the map below).
  2. Connect the black lead of the 9 V source to the electrode marked 0 V, and the red lead to the electrode marked 9 V. Clip firmly onto the copper tape.
  3. Never let the two leads touch each other.

b · Check the source (student 2)

  1. Set the voltmeter (multimeter) to DC volts, on the 20 V range.
  2. Touch the black probe to the 0 V electrode and the red probe to the 9 V electrode. Write the reading.

c · Find the points, path by path (student 2)

  1. Keep the black probe pressed on the 0 V electrode the whole time.
  2. Put the tip of the red probe on the paper next to the 0 V electrode, and slide it slowly toward the 9 V electrode.
  3. Stop where the voltmeter reads 1.00 V. Look at the paper's grid and read the position of the probe tip (x across, y up).
  4. Mark that point on the map below (student 1): choose the path and the voltage, then click the same spot. The box in the corner of the map shows where you are; zoom the page if you need more precision, or type x and y.
  5. Keep sliding toward the 9 V electrode and mark 2 V, 3 V … 8 V the same way. That is one path.
  6. Do 5 different paths in this arrangement. Spread them out: go straight, go around the sides, go above and below. Then move the leads to the next arrangement.
Each arrangement needs 5 paths with at least 6 of the 8 voltages (1 V to 8 V). Points very close to a small electrode can be hard to find: if you can't find one, skip it.
4

Join the points with the same voltage

All the points marked 3 V, for example, have the same potential. A line through them is an equipotential line: anywhere along it, the voltmeter reads 3 V.

  1. Choose a voltage, then draw a smooth line through all the points of that voltage: press, drag and release.
  2. Join points of the same voltage only. Don't zigzag to hit every point exactly: draw the smooth curve that passes closest to them.
  3. You may continue a line past your last point if you think it goes on. A line that comes out wrong: Erase it and draw it again.
  4. Draw the lines in all four arrangements, for at least 5 voltages in each.
Describe the shape of the equipotential lines in each arrangement (A, B, C and D). Where are they close together, and where are they far apart?

Predict the electric field

The electric field pushes a positive charge from one electrode toward the other. Discuss with your partner, then draw how you think the electric field lines go in each arrangement.

  1. Draw each field line in the direction the field points: start where you think the field comes from and end where it goes. Arrows appear in the direction you drew.
  2. Draw at least 3 field lines in each of the four arrangements.
Which way do your field lines point, and how did you decide? How are they related to the equipotential lines you drew?
In each arrangement, where do you think the field is strongest? Why?
6

How field lines and equipotential lines meet

Read this together. It is how physicists draw electric fields from equipotential lines.

1 · They always cross at right angles

A field line crosses every equipotential line at 90°. Moving a charge along an equipotential line doesn't change its potential, so the field does no work on it there: the field has no part along the line, only across it.

2 · The field points downhill

Field lines go from higher potential to lower potential: they leave the 9 V electrode and arrive at the 0 V electrode.

3 · Close lines, strong field

Where the equipotential lines are close together, the potential changes quickly and the field is strong. Its size is about E = ΔV / Δd: the voltage difference between two lines divided by the distance between them.

4 · Electrodes are equipotentials too

All the copper of one electrode has the same potential, so field lines leave and reach an electrode perpendicular to its edge.

Now draw the field again, following the rules

Your prediction stays on the map as a dashed purple line. Draw new field lines in white: from the 9 V electrode to the 0 V electrode, crossing every equipotential line at 90°. At least 3 in each arrangement.

Compare your new field lines with your prediction. What was right, and what did you change?

Reflect

a · How strong is the field between the strips?

In arrangement D, choose two of your equipotential lines between the strips, and measure the distance between them along a field line (straight across, near the middle). Use the coordinates on the map or the grid on your paper.

Then E = ΔV / Δd with Δd in meters.

Is the field between the two strips the same everywhere? Use the spacing of your equipotential lines as evidence. What happens near the ends of the strips?

b · Outside the ring

Connect arrangement A again. Keep the black probe on the ring and touch the red probe on the paper outside the ring, at two or three places.

Explain that reading. What is the electric field outside the ring? Use equipotential lines and field lines in your answer.

c · Looking back

Where on your four maps is the field strongest, and where is it weakest? How can you tell just by looking at the equipotential lines?
The voltmeter measures potential, not field. Explain how, with only a voltmeter, you were able to find the electric field.

📋 Lab guide: Equipotential Lines

Read this together before you start. It tells you what you'll do in this lab; the detailed instructions are inside each step of the report. You can open it again at any moment with the 📋 Lab guide button at the top.

Goal

Map the potential on a sheet of conductive paper between two electrodes, for four different arrangements. Join the points with the same voltage into equipotential lines, and use them to find the electric field.

The four arrangements

What you'll do

  1. Build (step 2): copy the four arrangements onto your paper with copper tape.
  2. Measure (step 3): one of you connects the 9 V source, the other finds the points where the voltmeter reads 1 V, 2 V … 8 V. Mark them on the map in this report: 5 paths in each arrangement.
  3. Join (step 4): draw the equipotential lines through the points with the same voltage.
  4. Predict (step 5): draw how you think the electric field goes.
  5. Learn the rule (step 6): how field lines and equipotential lines meet, and draw the field again.
  6. Reflect, conclude and send (steps 7 to 9).

Materials

Each group
  • A sheet of conductive paper with a centimeter grid
  • Copper tape (conductive on both sides) and scissors
  • A 9 V source (battery or power supply) with a red and a black lead with clips
  • A voltmeter or multimeter with two probes
  • A computer with this page
Tips
  • Press the probe tip gently on the paper: don't scratch or pierce it.
  • Wait a second for the reading to settle before you mark a point.
  • Read positions from the grid on the paper, with your eyes straight above the probe tip.
Take care: 9 V is safe to touch, but never connect the red and black leads directly to each other: the battery heats up and runs down. Disconnect the source when you finish.
